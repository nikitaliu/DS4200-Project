Exploring Massachusetts Housing Costs

A visual guide to understanding why housing costs what it does across Massachusetts towns. We combine listing data, town income data, and environmental risk scores to explain price differences in plain English.

Xinyue Du Xiqiao Liu Lin Pan April 2026

Introduction

Introduction

This project is an interactive explainer about housing prices across Massachusetts. We use public listing data and U.S. Census town data to show how prices change from one place to another, what home features matter most, and where affordability is under the most pressure.

Housing matters because prices in Massachusetts have risen far faster than many households can comfortably absorb. In Greater Boston, the income needed to buy a starter home rose from about $97,941 in 2021 to about $162,224 in 2025. That gap affects where people can live, how far they commute, and how long it takes to build financial stability.

Research Questions

Research Questions

  1. How do housing prices vary geographically across Massachusetts?
  2. What property features, such as size, age, and home type, have the strongest relationship with price?
  3. Do towns with better walkability and transit access have higher housing costs?
  4. Are environmental risks such as flood, fire, and heat reflected in housing prices, or are they being overlooked?

Methodology

Methodology

Data sources

We combined a Massachusetts housing listing dataset from Kaggle (8,726 listings after cleaning) with town-level median household income and population data from the U.S. Census Bureau ACS 2023 5-year estimates. The listing data includes price, square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, property type, walk/bike/transit scores, and five environmental risk dimensions.

Data cleaning

We converted text prices to numbers, standardized property types, removed duplicate listing IDs, filtered out non-Massachusetts rows, and capped extreme outliers above $10 million or 15,000 square feet. This left us with a clean, analysis-ready sample of 8,726 listings.

Engineered features

We created new columns to make prices easier to compare: price per square foot, price-to-income ratio (how many years of local income equals the median home price), estimated cap rate (a rough measure of rental return on investment), and composite scores for livability and environmental risk. After joining with Census data, we matched income information for 328 towns.

Findings

Findings

Research Question 1

How do prices vary across Massachusetts?

Interactive choropleth map of town-level housing pressure

Hover to compare towns, change the map metric with the dropdown, and click any town to open its profile panel with housing, demographic, and employment details.

The highest prices concentrate in coastal and island communities like Nantucket and Martha’s Vineyard, and in inner-ring suburbs such as Wellesley and Weston, while western Massachusetts is notably cheaper. Location is the strongest broad filter on affordability before buyers even compare individual homes.

Research Question 2

Which home features are most closely tied to price?

Scatter plot of price versus property features

Switch the x-axis feature, filter by property type, and brush a region to create a custom comparison set of comparable listings.

Price distribution by property type

The box shows the middle 50% of prices (the interquartile range, or IQR). Dots beyond the whiskers are unusually priced outliers.

Feature importance for listing price

This ranked bar chart summarizes which home features are most strongly associated with listing price after putting the variables on the same standardized scale.

The scatter plot works like a comparable-sales view: it shows that listing price rises most clearly with square footage and bathrooms, while bedrooms add signal but not as strongly. Filtering by property type helps separate condo, single-family, townhouse, and multi-family markets so viewers are not comparing unlike homes.

The D3 box plot adds the segment story. Single-family homes sit at the highest median price and also show the widest spread, which suggests more price uncertainty inside that segment. Condos and townhouses cluster into tighter bands, making them easier to compare within type.

The ranked feature-importance chart gives the cleanest direct answer to Research Question 2: square footage and bathrooms are the strongest positive price drivers in this dataset, while age and environmental risk matter less once the major home features are considered together. Taken as a set, these three views show that buyers are paying for both the physical scale of a home and the market segment it belongs to.

Research Question 3

Do walkability and transit access come with a premium?

Livability scores by price quartile

We split listings into four equal price groups (quartiles) and compared their average walk, bike, and transit scores across each group.

Town income versus median listing price

The dashed line marks a simple rule-of-thumb threshold where a home costs four times local income. Towns above that line are more stretched.

More expensive listings consistently score higher on walkability, bikeability, and transit access — convenience is priced in. Meanwhile, the income-versus-price chart shows that the majority of Massachusetts towns sit above the traditional 4× income affordability benchmark, revealing a statewide gap, not just a Greater Boston problem.

Research Question 4

Are environmental risks showing up in prices?

Correlation heatmap of prices, features, livability, and risk

Red cells mean two variables tend to move in the same direction. Blue cells mean they tend to move in opposite directions. Cells with stronger correlations are labeled directly.

The correlation between price and composite environmental risk is weakly positive rather than strongly negative, suggesting that hazard exposure is not yet fully priced into the market. Some desirable coastal locations remain expensive despite elevated flood, fire, or heat risk — meaning the true long-term cost of living there may be higher than the listing price implies.

Summary

Summary

This project explored housing prices across Massachusetts using 8,726 cleaned listings and Census income data for 328 towns. We found four main patterns: housing costs cluster heavily by geography, with coastal and inner-suburban towns far outpacing western Massachusetts; square footage and bathrooms are the strongest property-level price drivers; walkability and transit access come with a clear price premium; and environmental risks such as flood and fire are not discounted in listing prices — likely because the riskiest areas are also the most desirable coastal locations. These findings suggest that Massachusetts housing affordability is a statewide challenge shaped by location desirability, not just property size or condition.

Future Directions

Future Directions

Deeper housing detail

We would add time-series price data, school quality, and a cleaner rent dataset so the return and affordability measures rely less on proxy calculations.

More local context

We would also push below the town level for places like Boston, where neighborhood differences are large enough that a citywide average can hide important patterns.

One important limitation is that listing prices are asking prices, not closing prices. Another is that our cap rate and gross rent multiplier (GRM) values use a payment-based rent proxy rather than observed rent rolls.
