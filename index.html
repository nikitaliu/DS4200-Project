Introduction

A plain-English look at how Massachusetts housing works

This project is an interactive explainer about housing prices across Massachusetts. We use public listing data and U.S. Census town data to show how prices change from one place to another, what home features matter most, and where affordability is under the most pressure.

Housing matters because prices in Massachusetts have risen far faster than many households can comfortably absorb. In Greater Boston, the income needed to buy a starter home rose from about $97,941 in 2021 to about $162,224 in 2025. That gap affects where people can live, how far they commute, and how long it takes to build financial stability.

We wrote this site for the general public, not for specialists. That means every figure has a takeaway paragraph, formulas are explained in the Terminology section, and technical ideas like correlation or cap rate are introduced in everyday language.

Research Questions

What questions does this project answer?

  1. How do housing prices vary geographically across Massachusetts?
  2. What property features, such as size, age, and home type, have the strongest relationship with price?
  3. Do towns with better walkability and transit access have higher housing costs?
  4. Are environmental risks such as flood, fire, and heat reflected in housing prices, or are they being overlooked?

Methodology

What we used, how we cleaned it, and what we created

Data sources

What we did: We combined a Massachusetts housing listing dataset with town-level Census data so we could compare local prices with local income and demographics. How we did it: We started with a Kaggle dataset containing Zillow-style listing records, then called the 2023 ACS API for town-level income, tenure, race, home value, and employment-by-industry variables. The output: We matched income data for -- towns and generated one town profile file for the slide-in map panel.

Data cleaning

What we did: We cleaned raw listing fields so they could be used in calculations and charts. How we did it: We converted prices like “$999,000” into numbers, standardized property types, parsed score strings, removed duplicate listing IDs, filtered out non-Massachusetts rows, and capped extreme outliers above $10 million or 15,000 square feet. The output: We started with -- raw rows, removed -- duplicate IDs, filtered out -- non-Massachusetts rows, and finished with -- cleaned listings.

Feature engineering

What we did: We created new columns that make prices easier to compare. How we did it: We calculated values like price per square foot, price-to-income ratio, monthly affordability index, estimated cap rate, ROI, and composite scores for livability and environmental risk. The output: The final cleaned file includes more than 10 analytical features and supports every chart on the website.

Kaggle housing data

The raw listing file includes prices, square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, property type, estimated monthly payment, walkability, school distance, and five environmental risk fields.

ACS town profiles

The ACS pull provides median household income, owner-versus-renter counts, Census median home value, race or ethnicity counts, age buckets, and employment by industry for each town.

Terminology / Glossary

Key terms used throughout the site

Listing Price

The asking price shown in a home listing. It is what the seller hopes to get, not necessarily the final sale price.

Price per Square Foot

How much each square foot of living space costs. It helps compare homes of different sizes more fairly.

Formula: Price ÷ Square Footage

Example: --

Price-to-Income Ratio

How many years of a town’s typical household income equal the median home price. Higher values mean the market is less affordable.

Formula: Median Listing Price ÷ Median Household Income

Example: --

Monthly Affordability Index

Whether a typical household can cover a typical monthly housing payment. Values above 1 suggest the payment is more reachable; values below 1 suggest strain.

Formula: Monthly Household Income ÷ Estimated Monthly Payment

Estimated Cap Rate

A rough estimate of annual rental return on a property, shown as a percentage. In this project it is only a proxy, not observed rent.

Formula: (Estimated Annual Rent ÷ Price) × 100

Example: --

Gross Rent Multiplier (GRM)

How many years of rent would equal the purchase price. Lower values usually indicate better value for someone evaluating rental potential.

Formula: Price ÷ Estimated Annual Rent

Example: --

ROI (Return on Investment)

The percentage gain or loss compared with a property’s past purchase price. We estimate it from listing history when a past sale is available.

Formula: (Current Price − Past Price) ÷ Past Price × 100

Example: --

Annualized Appreciation

The average yearly percentage change in value since the last sale. It smooths multi-year price growth into a yearly rate.

Formula: (Current ÷ Past)^(1/years) − 1

Example: --

Livability Composite Score

An average of walk, bike, and transit scores. Higher values suggest it is easier to get around without depending entirely on a car.

Formula: (Walk + Bike + Transit) ÷ 3

Environmental Risk Composite

An average of flood, fire, wind, heat, and air-quality risk scores. Higher values mean greater environmental exposure.

Formula: (Flood + Fire + Wind + Heat + Air) ÷ 5

Correlation Coefficient (r)

A number from −1 to +1 that shows how strongly two things move together. Positive values mean they tend to rise together, and negative values mean one tends to fall as the other rises.

Quartile

Splitting all homes into four equal-sized price groups. Q1 is the cheapest quarter of listings and Q4 is the most expensive quarter.

IQR (Interquartile Range)

The distance between Q1 and Q3. Box plots use it to show the middle 50% of prices, and outliers sit beyond 1.5 times this range.

Findings

What the charts show

Each finding below answers one of the project’s research questions. The goal is not just to show a chart, but to explain what it means for people comparing where they might be able to live.

Research Question 1

How do prices vary across Massachusetts?

Interactive choropleth map of town-level housing pressure

Hover to compare towns, change the map metric with the dropdown, and click any town to open its profile panel with housing, demographic, and employment details.

We mapped the median listing price for towns across Massachusetts and added toggles for price-to-income ratio, estimated cap rate, and environmental risk. We found that the highest-price clusters are concentrated in coastal, island, and inner-metro towns, while the lowest-price towns sit largely in western and more rural parts of the state. This matters because location still acts as the strongest broad filter on affordability before buyers even start comparing individual houses.

Research Question 2

Which home features are most closely tied to price?

Scatter plot of price versus property features

Switch the x-axis feature, brush a region to create a custom comparison set, and use the density toggle if the dots become crowded.

Price distribution by property type

The box shows the middle 50% of prices. See IQR in the glossary for the exact definition.

We plotted listing price against home size, age, and other features, then grouped the listings by property type in a D3 box plot. We found that larger homes and homes with more bathrooms have the clearest positive relationship with price, while different property types occupy noticeably different price ranges and spreads. This means buyers are not only paying for “more space”; they are also paying for different market segments with different typical price bands.

Research Question 3

Do walkability and transit access come with a premium?

Livability scores by price quartile

We split listings into four quartiles by price and compared their average walk, bike, and transit scores.

Town income versus median listing price

The dashed line marks a simple rule-of-thumb threshold where a home costs four times local income. Towns above that line are more stretched.

We compared livability scores across price groups and then compared town income with town-level prices. We found that higher-priced groups generally score better on walkability and transit, but many towns still sit far above a traditional affordability threshold when local income is brought into the picture. This means convenience and accessibility do appear to raise prices, and households often have to choose between being well connected and staying within budget.

Research Question 4

Are environmental risks showing up in prices?

Correlation heatmap of prices, features, livability, and risk

Red cells mean two variables tend to move in the same direction. Blue cells mean they tend to move in opposite directions. Cells with stronger correlations are labeled directly.

We calculated how strongly each variable pair moves together using a correlation coefficient. We found that environmental risk is not strongly discounted in this sample: the overall relationship between price and environmental risk is weakly positive rather than strongly negative. This matters because some desirable locations may stay expensive even when they carry more long-term exposure, which can make the true cost of housing less obvious to the public.

Future Directions

What we would improve with more time

Deeper housing detail

We would add time-series price data, school quality, and a cleaner rent dataset so the return and affordability measures rely less on proxy calculations.

More local context

We would also push below the town level for places like Boston, where neighborhood differences are large enough that a citywide average can hide important patterns.

One important limitation is that listing prices are asking prices, not closing prices. Another is that our cap rate and GRM values use a payment-based rent proxy rather than observed rent rolls.
