Exploring Massachusetts Housing Costs

A visual guide to understanding why housing costs what it does across Massachusetts towns. We combine listing data, town income data, and environmental risk scores to explain price differences in plain English.

Xinyue Du Xiqiao Liu Lin Pan April 2026

Introduction

A plain-English look at how Massachusetts housing works

This project is an interactive explainer about housing prices across Massachusetts. We use public listing data and U.S. Census town data to show how prices change from one place to another, what home features matter most, and where affordability is under the most pressure.

Housing matters because prices in Massachusetts have risen far faster than many households can comfortably absorb. In Greater Boston, the income needed to buy a starter home rose from about $97,941 in 2021 to about $162,224 in 2025. That gap affects where people can live, how far they commute, and how long it takes to build financial stability.

We wrote this site for the general public, not for specialists. Every figure has a plain-English takeaway, and technical terms are explained inline where they first appear.

Research Questions

What questions does this project answer?

  1. How do housing prices vary geographically across Massachusetts?
  2. What property features, such as size, age, and home type, have the strongest relationship with price?
  3. Do towns with better walkability and transit access have higher housing costs?
  4. Are environmental risks such as flood, fire, and heat reflected in housing prices, or are they being overlooked?

Methodology

How we built this analysis

Data sources

We combined a Massachusetts housing listing dataset from Kaggle (8,726 listings after cleaning) with town-level median household income and population data from the U.S. Census Bureau ACS 2023 5-year estimates. The listing data includes price, square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, property type, walk/bike/transit scores, and five environmental risk dimensions.

Data cleaning

We converted text prices to numbers, standardized property types, removed duplicate listing IDs, filtered out non-Massachusetts rows, and capped extreme outliers above $10 million or 15,000 square feet. This left us with a clean, analysis-ready sample of 8,726 listings.

Engineered features

We created new columns to make prices easier to compare: price per square foot, price-to-income ratio (how many years of local income equals the median home price), estimated cap rate (a rough measure of rental return on investment), and composite scores for livability and environmental risk. After joining with Census data, we matched income information for 328 towns.

Kaggle housing data

The raw listing file includes prices, square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, property type, walkability, bike, and transit scores, and five environmental risk fields (flood, fire, wind, heat, air quality).

U.S. Census ACS 2023

The Census pull provides median household income, owner-versus-renter counts, median home value, race and ethnicity counts, age buckets, and employment by industry for each Massachusetts town.

Findings

What the charts show

Each finding below answers one of the project’s research questions. The goal is not just to show a chart, but to explain what it means for people comparing where they might be able to live.

Research Question 1

How do prices vary across Massachusetts?

Interactive choropleth map of town-level housing pressure

Hover to compare towns, change the map metric with the dropdown, and click any town to open its profile panel with housing, demographic, and employment details.

We mapped the median listing price for every town in Massachusetts. The color shading ranges from light yellow for lower-priced towns to deep red for the most expensive — making it easy to spot where housing costs cluster. The highest prices concentrate in coastal and island communities like Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard, and in inner-ring Boston suburbs such as Wellesley and Weston. Western Massachusetts towns are notably cheaper.

This directly answers Research Question 1: location is the strongest broad filter on affordability before buyers even compare individual homes. Use the dropdown above to switch the map metric and explore how income pressure, investment returns, and environmental risk vary across the same geography.

Research Question 2

Which home features are most closely tied to price?

Scatter plot of price versus property features

Switch the x-axis feature, brush a region to create a custom comparison set, and use the density toggle if the dots become crowded.

Price distribution by property type

The box shows the middle 50% of prices (the interquartile range, or IQR). Dots beyond the whiskers are unusually priced outliers.

The scatter plot above lets you explore how price relates to individual home features — switch the x-axis to compare square footage, number of bedrooms, bathrooms, or age. The clearest pattern is size: larger homes command higher prices, especially above 2,000 square feet. Bathrooms also track closely with price. Age is more mixed — a well-maintained older home can hold its value just as well as a newer build.

The box plot below groups listings by property type. Each box covers the middle 50% of prices for that category — called the interquartile range (IQR). Single-family homes have the widest price spread and the highest median; condos cluster lower with less variation. Dots beyond the whiskers represent unusual outliers. Together, both charts show that buyers pay for physical features and the type of home, not just raw square footage.

Research Question 3

Do walkability and transit access come with a premium?

Livability scores by price quartile

We split listings into four equal price groups (quartiles) and compared their average walk, bike, and transit scores across each group.

Town income versus median listing price

The dashed line marks a simple rule-of-thumb threshold where a home costs four times local income. Towns above that line are more stretched.

The grouped bar chart compares average walk, bike, and transit scores across four price groups, from the cheapest 25% of listings to the most expensive. Walk score — a 0–100 measure of how many daily errands can be done on foot — shows the sharpest jump between price groups. The trend is consistent across all three dimensions: more expensive homes are in more convenient locations. Convenience is priced in.

The income-versus-price chart plots each town's median household income against its median listing price. The dashed line marks a traditional affordability benchmark: a home priced at four times local annual income. Towns above the line are financially stretched — and the majority of Massachusetts towns fall above it. Even towns with well-above-average incomes are often not in affordable territory, showing that the housing affordability gap is a statewide challenge, not just a Greater Boston problem.

Research Question 4

Are environmental risks showing up in prices?

Correlation heatmap of prices, features, livability, and risk

Red cells mean two variables tend to move in the same direction. Blue cells mean they tend to move in opposite directions. Cells with stronger correlations are labeled directly.

We calculated how strongly each variable pair moves together using a correlation coefficient — a number from −1 to +1 where positive means two things tend to rise together and negative means one tends to fall as the other rises. We found that environmental risk is not strongly discounted in listing prices: the relationship between price and composite environmental risk is weakly positive rather than strongly negative.

This matters because it suggests that hazard exposure is not yet fully priced into the market. Some desirable locations remain expensive even when they carry elevated flood, fire, or heat risk — meaning the true long-term cost of living there may be higher than the listing price implies.

Future Directions

What we would improve with more time

Deeper housing detail

We would add time-series price data, school quality, and a cleaner rent dataset so the return and affordability measures rely less on proxy calculations.

More local context

We would also push below the town level for places like Boston, where neighborhood differences are large enough that a citywide average can hide important patterns.

One important limitation is that listing prices are asking prices, not closing prices. Another is that our cap rate and gross rent multiplier (GRM) values use a payment-based rent proxy rather than observed rent rolls.
